Every fellowship program eventually needs a video like this one: a direct, no-nonsense correction aimed at people who are doing good work but cutting corners on the presentation. Professor Bear opens by holding up real, anonymized emails sent back to Humanitarians AI Fellows whose video submissions weren't acceptable, not because the content was bad, but because the basics, a real description, decent audio, weren't there.

## What counts as a real YouTube description

The core complaint is simple: a one-line placeholder is not a YouTube description. Fellows are told the media team cannot be expected to write descriptions or titles for them, and that the standard is a description the team can simply copy and paste as-is, with no editing required. To make that achievable, fellows are pointed to a specific workaround: describe your video in a prompt to Claude, and it will generate a usable title and description. The instruction that follows matters just as much as the tool recommendation. Read what Claude gives you before submitting it, because you are the one who knows whether it is actually correct, not the AI and not the media team.

## Links belong in the description, not just in your head

Beyond a clear description, fellows are expected to include a link to the actual code behind their project. A LinkedIn link is treated as optional but strongly encouraged, framed not as vanity but as strategy: the fellowship exists as a bridge between graduation and a real job, so a video is also a chance to market yourself to anyone who watches it and wants to learn more about who made it.

## Fixing audio without fancy equipment

The second half of the video walks through actual audio review, watching submitted videos side by side and grading them out loud. One video is judged acceptable but not great; another is called out directly as too quiet to be usable. The fix demonstrated is a simple editing step: open the audio effects, find compression or amplification, and increase the level so the recording is more audible, then save it again. That's the entire fix. It does not require professional recording gear, just a few minutes in whatever editing tool is available, and a willingness to actually listen back to your own recording before submitting it.

## The expectation, stated plainly

Running through this checklist, Professor Bear repeats the same point from a few different angles: fellows made the video, so fellows are responsible for making it presentable. That includes writing a description that doesn't need editing, providing links to the code and (optionally) a LinkedIn profile, and checking that the audio is actually audible before it goes to the media team. None of these are difficult individually, but skipping all three is what generates the kind of email being read aloud at the start of the video.

## The Zoom watermark problem

One more issue gets flagged, though it is deliberately left for a separate, dedicated video: the persistent on-screen artifact that shows up throughout a recording when Zoom is used without adjusting its settings. The advice given here is practical rather than prescriptive. If you don't know how to remove it, take a screenshot of the issue and ask your favorite language model how to fix it. Zoom itself is treated as an entirely acceptable recording tool for fellows without access to more advanced software like OBS Studio, but leaving a visible watermark artifact sitting in the video for its entire runtime is called out as unacceptable, regardless of what tool was used to record it.

## Key takeaways

- A real YouTube description must be complete enough that the media team can copy and paste it without editing; a one-line placeholder does not qualify.
- Fellows can use Claude to draft a title and description by describing their video in a prompt, but must read and verify the output themselves before submitting.
- Every submission should include a link to the project's code, and a LinkedIn link is recommended as a way to market yourself during the fellowship.
- Low or muffled audio can usually be fixed with a simple amplification or compression effect in your editing software before you export and submit.
- Zoom is an acceptable recording tool, but visible watermark artifacts left in the final video are not; ask a language model for help removing them if you're unsure how.
