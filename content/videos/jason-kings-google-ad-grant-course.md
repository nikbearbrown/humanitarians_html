Google gives nonprofits a monthly ad grant, then makes it genuinely hard to use well. That gap is what created a market for Jason King's Google Ad Grant course, a paid training built specifically around navigating Google's byzantine nonprofit ad system. This is an honest, in-progress account of taking that course from the perspective of someone with a PhD in computer science and a background in AI, statistics, and computational biology, who still found it a lot to get through.

## First impressions: this is a lot of course

The course costs $200, and the first reaction to opening it up was simple: this thing is long. Longer, in terms of sheer volume of material, than graduate courses in quantum physics or theoretical computer science. That's not a knock on the content so much as an observation about scope. Estimating how long it would actually take to complete, the answer came back around 40 hours, doable in about eight weeks for a busy person working through it steadily. For a nonprofit team trying to get a grant account functional, that's a serious time investment before any ads even go live.

## Why the course is worth it anyway

Even acknowledging the length, the underlying judgment is that Jason King is clearly passionate about helping nonprofits navigate this specific problem, and per-minute, the course isn't overpriced. What makes it valuable isn't hand-holding on the basics, like explaining what Google Ad Grants is or how to sign up. It's that once you skip past those fundamentals, King's material reflects real hands-on expertise navigating Google's specific and often unintuitive nonprofit rules, the kind of detail you can't easily piece together from Google's own documentation.

## Condensing 40 hours into 8

Rather than working straight through the course front to back, the approach here was to ask an AI assistant which modules actually mattered for someone who already understands the basics of Google Ads and just needs the nonprofit-specific and grant-specific parts. The AI-suggested sequence came out to roughly 8 to 10 hours total, a fraction of the full course length, focused specifically on the parts that matter once the fundamentals are already understood. That's the core strategy: use the course as a reference to mine for grant-specific expertise, not a class to be sat through linearly.

## A concrete lesson already applied: one campaign, not three

One specific piece of advice from the course had already changed a live campaign structure by the time of this review. The original plan was three separate campaigns. King's material flagged that as a mistake, for reasons that are, true to form, non-intuitive from a general advertising standpoint. Google's own instinct would suggest more campaigns should help relevancy, since campaign structure and site structure both signal what each campaign is about. But the better approach, per King's guidance, is one campaign with ad groups underneath it for each specific sub-topic, rather than a bunch of separate campaigns.

## Why one campaign actually simplifies things

That restructuring turns out to make operational sense once you see how Google spends the daily budget. A campaign gets a budget, and Google decides among all the ad groups under that campaign how to allocate it. Set a budget of $320 a day, and Google will effectively try to spend close to that, sometimes slightly over, which isn't really a problem since it means the nonprofit's ad grant money was the thing being spent, not the organization's own funds. The actual risk nonprofits face isn't overspending their own money, it's the reverse: Google allocating a nominal budget of $320 a day but the account structure only letting $2 of it actually get used, because of exactly the kind of structural issue this course exists to prevent.

## The honest critique

Despite the value, there's a clear critique here: the course would benefit from a condensed, essentials-only track alongside the full version, something built for people who care more about their time than about watching 50 hours of video. That's not a dismissal of King's expertise, it's a request for a second format that respects how differently people can absorb the same information depending on their starting point and their bandwidth.
