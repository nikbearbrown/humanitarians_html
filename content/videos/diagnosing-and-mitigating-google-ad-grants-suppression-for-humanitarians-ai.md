A nonprofit Google Ad Grants account is supposed to hand out up to $10,000 a month in free advertising. Getting it to actually show a single ad, it turns out, is a separate problem entirely. This video documents that problem in real time: a properly configured campaign for Humanitarians AI that produced zero impressions, not zero clicks, zero impressions, over three straight days.

## A grant that isn't granting anything yet

Professor Bear starts by laying out what was actually done to set up the campaign. Following Google's own rules meant building highly specific, targeted landing pages, one for each real program Humanitarians AI runs, with content that closely matches the keywords being bid on. That effort paid off in Google's own scoring: the campaign hit a 99.9% relevancy score. Around 2,000 to 3,000 keywords were set up around those pages. And after three days, the account had generated zero impressions, meaning the ads weren't even being shown, let alone clicked.

## A blunt critique of Google's interface

A large part of the video is a direct critique of how difficult the Ad Grants setup process is to navigate, especially for a company Google's size. The core complaint is about interface design: a nonprofit user should be able to describe their organization and goals in a chatbot-style interface and have the platform guide them to an optimal campaign setup. Instead, the process requires piecing together scattered settings with no clear signal about what's missing or misconfigured, a level of complexity that, in the video's framing, shouldn't exist at a company with Google's resources.

## Turning to Gemini's deep research

Rather than staying stuck, the next step was using Google's own Gemini deep research tool to investigate the zero-impressions problem directly, asking it to explain why a properly configured, highly relevant campaign wasn't generating any impressions at all. That research surfaced a specific candidate explanation: something called "Smart Bidding," a term the video is upfront about not yet understanding in any depth. The plan going forward is to research exactly what Smart Bidding is, confirm whether it's enabled or missing on the account, and figure out how to set it up correctly if it isn't.

## The keyword volume problem

A second issue surfaces alongside the Smart Bidding question: keyword volume. Many of the keywords set up for the campaign are so low-volume that Google's own system won't accept some of them as biddable at all, since Google's platform is built around search volume that's orders of magnitude larger than what a small nonprofit's specific programs generate. The video notes a willingness to work within that constraint, keeping low-volume keywords active and accepting a click here and there, as long as total ad spend uses up the available $10,000 monthly grant, rather than expecting the account to behave like a high-volume commercial campaign.

## What comes next

The video closes without a resolution, framed explicitly as a status update rather than a fix. The next planned step is researching what Smart Bidding actually does and how to configure it properly, then reporting back on whether turning it on changes anything, since going from zero impressions to any impressions at all would already be an improvement over the current state.

## Key takeaways

- A campaign built to Google's own specifications, with a 99.9% relevancy score and roughly 2,000 to 3,000 keywords, produced zero impressions over three days.
- Google's Gemini deep research tool was used to investigate the problem and surfaced "Smart Bidding" as a likely missing piece.
- It's unclear whether Smart Bidding was already enabled by default or needs to be manually configured, and that's the next research step.
