Teaching a three-year-old the word "vocabulary" doesn't work. Teaching them a rhyme they'll hum for the rest of the day does. That's the whole strategy behind this song: instead of listing the five senses as facts to memorize, it turns each one into a rhyming couplet a child can sing back within a single listen.

## Building each sense around something a child already knows

Every verse leans on concrete, everyday images rather than abstract description. Sight is "the sun so bright," stars at night, a teddy bear, the people around you. Hearing is a bird singing, a bell going "ding-a-ling," music loud and clear. Smell is cookies baking and flowers, with the honest acknowledgment that "some smell good and some not." Taste covers pie and French fries, sweet and sour, hot and cold. Touch is a fuzzy teddy bear, chilly snow, and a warm, cozy blanket. None of these examples require explanation; a preschooler has already lived every one of them, which is what makes the vocabulary stick.

## Repetition as the actual lesson

The chorus, "I can see, I can hear, I can smell things and I can taste, I can touch, I can do and learn so much," bookends the song and repeats the full list of senses twice. That repetition is doing more pedagogical work than any single verse. Naming all five senses together, more than once, is what turns five separate facts into one connected idea: that the body has multiple ways of taking in the world, and a child can name every one of them.

## Designed for the classroom, not just the living room

The song is built for circle time and group settings as much as solo listening. Its short, punchy lines are easy for a group of children to echo back, which matters for classroom use, ESL instruction, and any setting where a teacher wants call-and-response rather than passive listening. The simple, high-frequency vocabulary also makes it accessible to children who are still building their English vocabulary, not just native speakers learning the concept for the first time.
