Seven hours spent inside Google Ad Grants for a nonprofit produced exactly zero impressions. Not zero clicks, zero impressions. That result, combined with an interface that seemed determined to hide its own basic functions, led to a pointed question put directly to Google's own Gemini: do idiots work at Google?

## Seven hours, zero impressions

The goal was straightforward: get Google Ad Grants working for Humanitarians AI, a nonprofit that had already built a fast, analytics-equipped, quality-content website meeting every stated guideline. Despite following every recommendation, the campaign produced no impressions at all after seven hours of setup and troubleshooting with Gemini's help.

## An interface that fights the user

Part of the frustration wasn't the advertising system itself but the interface built around it. Critical menu items were tucked behind a tiny back button rendered in roughly 8-point font, in a layout where the obvious move, a large plus button, doesn't lead where you'd expect. Even Gemini, while assisting with navigation, was reportedly complaining about the interface along the way. That's a notable detail: the AI tool meant to help troubleshoot the platform was itself struggling with how the platform was built.

## Asking Gemini directly

After getting the standard "cold start problem" explanation for the lack of impressions, and knowing that cold start problems are a well-understood challenge in machine learning with plenty of established solutions, the follow-up question was pointed: do idiots work at Google? Gemini's answer was direct: no, they're not idiots. They're arguably worse. They just don't care.

## Why that answer rings true

The reasoning behind accepting that answer comes down to incentives. If this were a paid Google Ads campaign instead of a free grant, the experience would almost certainly be flawless, because Google would risk losing paying customers over an experience this broken. But Google Ad Grants operates differently. Google can likely write off the $10,000 per year in grant value whether a nonprofit ever successfully uses it or not, which means there's little financial incentive to make the free tier actually work well.

The distinction drawn here matters: bad UX is fairly typical for Google generally. But bad UX combined with an unsolved cold start problem, on a system built by a company that literally teaches the industry standard approaches to solving cold start problems, is not a technical limitation. It's a choice not to prioritize fixing it, specifically for the free nonprofit tier.
