Verb conjugation tables are one of the least musical things a language learner ever has to memorize, and they're usually where beginning Italian students stall out first. This song takes the three verbs almost every Italian sentence eventually needs, essere, avere, and stare, and sets their full conjugations to an acoustic folk melody, stripped of on-screen captions so the ear has to do the work instead of the eyes.

## Why these three verbs

Essere means "to be," avere means "to have," and stare means "to stay" or "to feel." Together they cover an enormous share of everyday Italian: who you are, what you have, and how you feel or where you are staying. The song's own framing captures why they matter together: they help you say who you are, what you have, and how you feel, which is close to the full range of basic self-expression in the language.

## Essere: "to be," conjugated in full

The first verse works through every form: io sono (I am), tu sei (you are), lui è (he is), lei è (she is), noi siamo (we are), voi siete (you all are), loro sono (they are). The lyric example "Io sono un sogno," I am a dream, shows the verb in a real sentence rather than as an isolated table entry, and the chorus reduces the whole verb down to a hook: essere, I am, you are.

## Avere: "to have," and everyday uses

The second verse conjugates avere the same way: io ho (I have), tu hai (you have), lui ha (he has), lei ha (she has), noi abbiamo (we have), voi avete (you all have), loro hanno (they have). The example lines, "got a book, got a cat, say ho un libro just like that," tie the verb to concrete, everyday objects, and the song notes that avere is also used for age in Italian, a detail that trips up a lot of English speakers used to using "to be" for age instead.

## Stare: "to stay" or "to feel"

The bridge covers stare: io sto (I stay or I feel), tu stai, lui sta, lei sta, noi stiamo, voi state, loro stanno. The lyric "sto bene," I'm doing fine, and "stiamo qui," we've got time, show two of the verb's most common everyday uses, checking in on how someone feels and describing staying somewhere. Stare doesn't map onto a single English verb, which is exactly why hearing it repeated in context, rather than reading a translation once, helps it stick.
