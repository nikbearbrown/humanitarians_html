A sentence like "revenue is down this quarter because enterprise renewals slipped" can walk straight into a board deck sounding completely certain, and completely unverified, at the same time. That gap, between how confident a claim sounds and whether anyone actually checked it, is what Humanitarians AI Fellow Aishwarya Patil calls the fluency trap, and it is the subject of this short walkthrough on writing and auditing a variance note.

## The fluent-wrong note

The example is deliberately ordinary. A variance note explaining a revenue drop reads smoothly, uses the right financial vocabulary, and names a plausible cause. Nothing about the sentence signals that it has no source behind it. That is the trap: fluent language and sourced language are indistinguishable on the page. The confidence of the writing tells a reader nothing about whether the underlying number was ever checked against real data.

## What a variance note actually needs

Aishwarya's method treats a variance note as evidence only once it ties to three things: a source, a period, and an owner who can be asked how they know. Any sentence missing one of those is not yet evidence, no matter how polished it reads. This is the test to apply before anything from a draft goes into a deck, and it is meant to be run on every factual claim, not spot-checked on a few.

## Preparation versus judgment

The fix she describes is structural rather than a reminder to "be more careful." It is a phase gate that splits the workflow into two distinct roles. The machine, in this case an AI system, drafts the note, computes figures, and flags items that need attention. A named human then reads the flagged items, ties each one back to a source, and decides whether the note is actually ready to go out. That handoff point, where a specific person takes responsibility for a specific number, is where accountability enters the document. Without that named human step, the gate is decorative.

## What "verified" requires

The video sets a concrete bar for the word "verified" in a finance context. Four things need to exist together: a named source file, the period that file covers, a control total that ties to the general ledger, and an owner who will stand behind the number if asked. If any one of these four is missing, the sentence is an assertion, not evidence, regardless of how confident or technically fluent it sounds. This four-part test is what makes the fluency trap detectable in practice rather than just a concept to keep in mind.
