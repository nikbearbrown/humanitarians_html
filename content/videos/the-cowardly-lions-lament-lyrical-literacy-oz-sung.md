The Cowardly Lion has always been an odd kind of hero, a beast built for roaring who spends most of his story hiding from rustling leaves. This sung poem takes that contradiction and lets the Lion explain it himself, in his own voice, from the inside of his fear rather than as a punchline about it.

## A lion who can't stop shaking

The poem opens with the Lion alone in a forest, pacing, afraid to leap. His roar is loud, his mane is fine, but "fear made cowards of beasts like mine." That line does a lot of work: it separates the Lion's appearance, the roar and the mane that should signal courage, from his actual state, which is constant, shameful fear. A rustle sends him running. A breeze sends him into hiding. The poem is explicit that this isn't a lion who lacks the physical trappings of bravery. He has all of them and still can't act on them, which is the entire emotional hook of the piece.

## Joining a journey he doesn't feel ready for

The turn comes when he meets "a girl with storm-washed shoes," a tin man, and a straw-stuffed man, characters drawn straight from L. Frank Baum's The Wonderful Wizard of Oz, and they welcome him despite his shame. From there, the Lion follows them through poppy fields and haunted wood, and the poem is careful not to pretend his fear disappears. "Each step a quake, each choice a test, yet still I marched though not the best." He isn't transformed into a fearless creature. He's a frightened one who keeps walking anyway, which is closer to how courage actually works for most people than the version where fear simply vanishes.

## What he finds isn't the absence of fear

By the poem's end, the Lion kneels before the flame in Oz and receives, in his words, "a medal, not just fame, but proof I'd faced my deepest scare." The lesson the poem lands on is stated almost as plainly as a thesis: "courage is the act of near, the trembling breath, the shaky paw that walks through fear and stands in awe." Not the absence of trembling, but the willingness to move while trembling. The closing lines make the same point from a different angle: "cowardice is where I begin, and courage is what I carry in," framing courage not as a trait you either have or don't, but as something built through the act of continuing.

## Rooted in a story already in the public domain

This piece is explicitly framed as inspired by, not a retelling of, the Cowardly Lion from Baum's 1900 novel, in which the character joins Dorothy, the Scarecrow, and the Tin Woodman on the journey to the Emerald City to ask the Wizard for courage. The novel is a foundational work of American children's literature and entered the public domain in 1956, which is why it's fair territory for this kind of musical reinterpretation as part of the Lyrical Literacy podcast.
