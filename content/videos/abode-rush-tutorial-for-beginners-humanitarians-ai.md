Video editing software has a reputation for being intimidating to first-time users, packed with menus and options that take a professional editor years to fully master. Adobe Premiere Rush, part of the Adobe Creative Cloud suite, is built specifically to avoid that problem, and this walkthrough covers its core tools in the order a beginner would actually encounter them, from importing a first clip through exporting a finished video.

## Starting a project and getting familiar with the interface

Starting a new project in Rush begins from the homepage, where a "create new project" option lets a user select a video either from their local desktop or directly from cloud storage, with the option to select and merge multiple clips at once if needed. Once a video is selected and the project is created, a short processing step brings the footage into the workspace. From there, the interface's basic navigation includes a home button to return to the full project list, along with separate edit and share options, the share option being where a finished video eventually gets exported or published.

## Adding and organizing media

An "add media" option lets a user bring in additional clips or images alongside the original footage, pulled from the same media browser used to import the first clip. Beyond video, Rush also supports adding graphics, including new text overlays, audio files, and voiceovers directly into a project. All the media currently added to a project sits in a repository or storage bin, giving an editor a central place to see everything available to work with.

## Trimming and arranging footage

The core trimming tool is a scissors icon used to split a clip at the playhead's current position. Splitting a clip at two different points isolates a section in between, which can then either be duplicated using a dedicated duplicate icon, or removed entirely using a delete option, which cuts that section out of the final video cleanly.

## Working with audio separately from video

Rush includes an "expand audio" option that separates a clip's audio track from its video, since footage typically arrives as one combined file. Splitting them apart makes it much easier to edit audio independently, for example removing a section of audio without affecting the corresponding video, or removing audio entirely and replacing it with something else. A dedicated audio panel provides further control, including volume adjustment, background noise reduction for ambient sounds like traffic or an air conditioner, and speech enhancement options for adjusting pitch and intensity. Rush also includes built-in music that can be browsed and added directly to a project when a full soundtrack replacement is needed.

## Managing multiple tracks

A track control feature lets an editor work with multiple layers at once, useful when adding a new image or video element on top of existing footage. Individual tracks can be locked, to prevent accidental changes to a section that's already finished, or muted and unmuted, giving fine control over which audio elements are actually playing at any given point without having to delete anything.

## Titles, transitions, and reframing

A graphics and text option provides access to a library of pre-built titles and overlay templates that can be dropped directly into a project. A separate effects section offers a range of transitions, including dissolve, wipe, and slide styles, which can be applied between clips and trimmed to a specific duration; transitions are typically applied to both the audio and video tracks simultaneously, though the audio portion of a transition can be removed independently if it isn't wanted.

Rush also supports changing a video's aspect ratio, with options including 16:9, 9:16, 4:5, and 1:1, useful for adapting a single piece of footage across different platforms. Paired with that is an auto-reframe option, which attempts to automatically keep the main subject centered in the frame after an aspect ratio change. Auto-reframe doesn't always get it exactly right, and when it misses, a manual "adjust frame" option lets an editor pull the frame to the correct position by hand, working through the footage as needed to keep the subject properly framed throughout.

## Color, speed, and final adjustments

Rush includes a set of built-in presets and filters, along with a manual edit option for adjusting exposure, contrast, highlights, and shadows directly. A speed control option allows footage to be sped up or slowed into slow motion, with ramp options available to ease in and out of a speed change gradually rather than switching abruptly. A transform option handles cropping and resizing footage to a specific width and height, along with rotation, useful when working with still images pulled into a video sequence, and includes crop presets along with an edge feather option that softens the boundary of a crop.

## Exporting and sharing

Once editing is complete, the share button provides the final export options: saving the finished video locally with a custom filename and destination, or publishing directly to YouTube, Instagram, or Facebook from within the app. Export and publish time varies depending on the length of the video being processed.

## Key takeaways

- Adobe Premiere Rush projects start from either local or cloud-stored footage, with support for merging multiple clips into one project.
- The scissors tool splits clips at the playhead, and isolated sections can be duplicated or deleted directly.
- Audio can be separated from video using the expand audio option, enabling independent edits, background noise reduction, and pitch or intensity adjustments.
- Track controls allow locking finished sections and muting or unmuting audio without deleting content.
- Aspect ratio changes paired with auto-reframe, plus a manual adjust-frame fallback, help keep the main subject centered when converting footage between formats like 16:9 and 9:16.
