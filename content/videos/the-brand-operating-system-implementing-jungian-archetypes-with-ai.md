## Where the archetypes come from

Carl Jung called them archetypes: characters living in what he named the collective unconscious, not invented by any one ad agency, but shared across cultures. The hero, the sage, the caregiver, people recognize them before anyone formally defines them. Marketers use archetypes so a brand feels like one consistent person instead of a committee of competing voices.

## The Mark and Pearson wheel

Branding borrowed Jung's framework directly. Mark and Pearson mapped 12 archetypes for business use: the innocent, sage, explorer, hero, outlaw, magician, everyman, lover, jester, caregiver, creator, and ruler. The practice is to pick one primary archetype and name a shadow archetype alongside it. From that single choice, every downstream decision follows: copy tone, visual palette, and which features get emphasized.

## Why archetypes matter for consistency

An archetype functions as a shortcut to consistency across a brand. A sage doesn't joke the way a jester does. A hero challenges the audience; a caregiver protects it. When every touchpoint, ads, website, support, social, plays the same character, it's because the archetype is acting as an operating system underneath all of it, not just decoration on a single campaign.

## From workshop deck to daily practice

Without a tool enforcing it, archetypes tend to die in a workshop deck: chosen once, then forgotten. An AI archetype engine turns the theory into daily practice by doing three things: classifying which character the brand is playing in a given piece of copy, drafting new copy that stays in character, and flagging lines that drift off archetype before they ship. The distinction drawn here is sharp: a gimmick runs a personality quiz once and forgets about it; a real mechanism picks an archetype, writes forcing functions around it, and lets AI enforce those functions against every piece of copy going forward. If the archetype doesn't change what actually ships, it's decoration, not a system.
