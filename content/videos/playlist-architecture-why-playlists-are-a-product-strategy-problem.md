Most YouTube channels sort their playlists the same way they'd sort files on a hard drive: by project, by topic, by whatever internal category made sense when the video was uploaded. Sanjana, a project manager at Humanitarians AI, argues that this is the single biggest architectural mistake a channel can make, because a playlist isn't a filing system. It's a product.

## The folder instinct is the mistake

A folder answers one question: where does this video live? A playlist answers a completely different question: what does the viewer want to watch next? Right now, the Humanitarians AI channel files content by project, Mycroft, NeuroVEP, Raman, which is exactly the folder instinct at work. But every playlist should function as its own small product, with its own audience, its own entry point, and its own success metric. Organizing by viewer intent instead of by internal project name is the actual shift from filing to shipping.

## Three questions every playlist has to answer

A playlist earns the label "product" only if it can answer three specific questions. First, who is it for: a beginner, a practitioner, or a decision maker? Second, what does the viewer want after video one: reinforcement, contrast, depth, or application? Third, what should they do at the end: subscribe, watch another playlist, or take some action offline? A playlist that can't answer all three isn't a product. It's a pile.

## Using AI as a co-strategist, three ways a week

Claude gets used every week in three specific roles here, not as a vague brainstorming partner but for three distinct jobs.

**Clustering.** Real playlists get pasted into Claude with instructions to group them by viewer intent rather than by project. In practice, that means Claude and Lyrical Literacy content clusters for beginners; Mycroft, NeuroVEP, and Raman cluster for practitioners; and Fellows, Madison, and Medhavy cluster for decision makers. Wherever those AI-generated clusters disagree with the channel's existing playlist structure, that disagreement points directly at where the architecture is weak.

**Journey mapping.** For any given playlist, Claude is asked to write the viewer's inner monologue moving from video one to video two, then from two to three. If that transition reads as forced in the text Claude produces, it will feel forced to an actual viewer on the platform too.

**Gap analysis.** Claude is asked to name the playlist the channel should have but doesn't. The HAI playlist alone runs 141 videos deep, mixing shipped tools, learning topics, and one-off explainers into a single undifferentiated pile. Gap analysis surfaced three real playlists hiding inside that pile: HAI Tools That Shipped for decision makers, Learn AI for Good for beginners, and AI Explained for the curious.

## The payoff: one pile becomes three products

Watching that single 141-video pile split apart is the clearest demonstration of the whole idea. The same library of videos sorts into three genuinely different products, each built for a different viewer, without a single new video being made. That's the entire move: stop filing content by where it lives, and start shipping it as something built for who's watching.

## Why this ranks second after the videos themselves

The core claim here is that playlists are the second most important product decision on a channel, right behind the videos themselves. Treating a playlist as a product rather than a folder means using AI to pressure-test whether the current architecture actually serves a viewer's intent, or whether it just mirrors the channel's own internal filing habits.
