If Google Ad Grants gives nonprofits $10,000 a month in free advertising, what does Microsoft offer? Professor Bear went looking for a direct equivalent and came back with a more mixed answer, one worth understanding before any nonprofit spends time on the application.

## Does Microsoft have its own version of Ad Grants?

The short answer is no. Professor Bear checked the Microsoft site specifically to see whether they had something comparable to Google's advertising grant program, and they don't. What Microsoft offers instead is a different bundle of benefits, mostly built around discounts on their own product ecosystem: Microsoft 365, Copilot, and Dynamics. He created a page on the Humanitarians AI site laying out exactly what those benefits are, because most of them come down to getting a lower price on tools that pull an organization deeper into the Microsoft ecosystem rather than giving it resources it can deploy freely elsewhere.

## Weighing the AI training offer

One piece of the program is a set of AI training resources. Professor Bear is candid about not needing this: Humanitarians AI already provides AI training as its core mission, and he's wary that Microsoft's version of AI training would mostly teach people how to use Microsoft's own tools rather than AI concepts more broadly. Given that his organization already produces a large volume of free video content on YouTube covering exactly this kind of material, the training portion of Microsoft's offer doesn't add much value for an organization already in that space.

## The part that's actually useful: the Azure grant

The one piece of the program Professor Bear considers genuinely worth pursuing is the Azure credit: $2,000 a year. That's modest, and he says so directly, but it's still better than nothing, especially for an organization like Humanitarians AI where the Fellows program is built around learning by doing. Fellows build real projects, and having Azure credits available gives them cloud infrastructure to build on without personally absorbing the cost. That practical, hands-on framing is what makes the credit worth applying for even though the dollar amount is small.

## What the application actually requires

The application process itself is straightforward. Professor Bear describes giving Microsoft the organization's legal information and EIN. Proof of nonprofit status isn't strictly required, but he uploaded the IRS determination letter anyway, along with the organization's website, as supporting documentation. After submitting, the application moves into a review stage, where his expectation is that Microsoft is mainly double-checking that the submitted numbers and information are accurate before moving to a next step.

## An honest bottom line

Professor Bear's overall assessment is measured rather than enthusiastic. Most of what Microsoft offers doesn't add real value for an organization that already provides AI training and already produces educational content, and a lot of the program functions as a way to nudge nonprofits toward adopting more Microsoft products. But the Azure credit is real, free money toward infrastructure, and there's a chance the offering becomes more substantive over time. That's reason enough to apply even without high expectations.
