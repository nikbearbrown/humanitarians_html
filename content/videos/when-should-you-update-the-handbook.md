Someone at a company asks when they should update the employee handbook. The wrong first answer is a date on the calendar. Liam explains what actually should trigger an update, and why the obvious annual-review plan leaves a real gap.

## Why the yearly review leaves a gap

The obvious plan is a yearly review: read through the whole handbook every December and fix whatever looks stale. But a law that changes in March does not wait for December. The section it touches becomes wrong the day the law takes effect, and it stays wrong for however many months remain until the scheduled review catches up. The fix is not reviewing more often on a fixed schedule. It is tracking updates by what actually triggers them: a specific legal change.

## One law change, one section

Consider a state raising its paid-leave minimum in the spring. That single change touches exactly one section of the handbook, the leave policy, not the entire document. That section gets a specific fix: new language reflecting the new minimum, logged along with what changed, why it changed, and the date the change took effect. This creates a record instead of a silent, undocumented override of old language.

## Flagged is not the same as finished

A flagged section is not automatically a finished one. Drafted language addressing a legal change still needs a legal read before it ships to employees. And a quiet quarter with no flagged sections does not mean the handbook is complete or fully up to date, it only means no tracked law changed during that period, which is a meaningfully different claim.

## The real trigger for going out of date

A handbook does not go out of date on a birthday, the way a fixed annual schedule implies. It goes out of date the day a relevant law changes. Tracking updates by the law that triggers them, rather than by a date on the calendar, closes the gap that a purely scheduled review leaves open.

## Auditing your own handbook

A useful exercise is picking one policy in an employee handbook, leave, overtime, remote work, or anything else, and asking two questions: when was it actually last changed, and has any law touching that topic changed since then. If either question cannot be answered, that is the specific gap this law-triggered update habit is meant to close.

## Key takeaways

- A law that changes mid-year makes the affected handbook section wrong on the day it takes effect, not on the next scheduled review date.
- Tracking updates by specific legal triggers, rather than a fixed calendar schedule, closes the gap that annual review alone leaves open.
- A single legal change, like a state raising its paid-leave minimum, should trigger a fix to exactly the section it affects, not a full handbook rewrite.
- Every tracked change should be logged with what changed, why, and the effective date, creating a record instead of a silent override.
- A flagged section still needs legal review before it ships, and a quiet quarter with no flags means no tracked law changed, not that the handbook is complete.

## Who this is for

HR teams, compliance staff, and business owners responsible for maintaining an employee handbook who want a concrete method for keeping policies current with actual legal changes rather than relying solely on a fixed annual review.
