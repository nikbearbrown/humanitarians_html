Getting an AI-generated character to speak convincingly is one problem. Getting it to sing, switch emotional register, and hold a close-up without breaking is a different problem entirely. This animation test runs three distinct character designs, Marge, Ned, and Apu, through both speaking and singing performances using Hedra AI, treating the whole thing as a working experiment rather than a finished piece.

## Three looks, one test

The video sets up three visually distinct characters: one in an intense vintage diner setting, one built around bold color contrasts, and one with a friendly suburban aesthetic. Running three different visual styles side by side in a single test lets the creators see how each design holds up once it's actually animated and voiced, rather than judging a static character sheet. The test also calls out a specific detail worth watching for: the picket fence placed in front of Ned's character gets animated along with him, a small sign of how much of the frame the tool is actually tracking and moving.

## What makes the animation hold together

One clear takeaway from the test is about camera angle and framing. Front-facing characters with a close-up on the face tend to work best. That's a practical, technical finding rather than a stylistic preference, and it's the kind of detail that only shows up once you've actually run several character types through the same pipeline and compared results.

## Voice acting across very different material

The audio side of the test doesn't stick to one tone. It moves through a rap-style verse about not chasing flashy things, into a domestic back-and-forth about eating vegetables and wearing a sweater, and then into a Christmas carol passage about rejoicing with heart and soul and voice. That range is the point: the test is explicitly about how AI voice technology adapts across different visual tones, using pitch modulation for dramatic moments, clarity enhancement for stylized designs, and warmth projection for characters meant to feel approachable. Putting a rap verse, a casual exchange, and a carol through the same three character designs is a fast way to see where the voice and the animation stay in sync and where they start to pull apart.

## An early prototype, treated like one

The video is upfront about what it is: an early prototype in an ongoing exploration of AI animation capabilities, not a finished short. That framing matters. Testing pitch, clarity, and warmth against three very different character looks, and across three very different types of dialogue, is exactly the kind of groundwork that has to happen before any of it shows up in a polished production.
