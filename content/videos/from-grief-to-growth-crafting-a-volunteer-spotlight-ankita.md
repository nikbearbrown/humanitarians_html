The example is a spotlight, deliberately distinct from a product pitch: it centers a volunteer's own journey, not an organization's offering.

## The story itself

When Swatisama left Mumbai for Boston in 2022, she carried big dreams and, soon after, an unbearable loss. In her first month of grad school, her father passed away. Thousands of miles from family, grief felt like an ocean she could not cross. One day she picked up her guitar. The music that had comforted her as a child slowly brought her back to herself. She then found Lyrical Literacy, a Humanitarians AI project that turns words into songs to help children learn to read, and began volunteering. Writing songs for kids did not just help them; it helped her heal. Today she advocates for AI in education and accessibility.

## The arc underneath the story

The structure is named directly: Overcoming the Monster. The monster here is grief and isolation. The path runs through loss, being abroad alone, then music and volunteering, then healing, then advocacy. It's the same three-act spine familiar from countless stories, just told with care and grounded in a real, specific person's experience. The line that sticks, and does the emotional work of the piece, is that music was no longer just a comfort; it was a bridge between pain and purpose. That line is the growth beat and the proof the arc actually landed.

## Why it reads as a spotlight, not a pitch

Loss becomes isolation. Music becomes a bridge. Volunteering becomes purpose. The reader leaves with a feeling and a soft door to join, not a hard sell. That distinction matters for how these posts function: a spotlight exists to honor a volunteer's real experience and invite empathy, while a pitch exists to move a reader toward a specific action on behalf of the organization. Mixing the two undermines both.

## The five-point checklist

Deshmukh distills the craft into five reusable points: open on a scene, show real change, stay human, end soft, and get consent first. That checklist is what gets copied into every spotlight post going forward, regardless of whose story is being told.

## Keeping categories clean

One organizational note matters as much as the craft: Swatisama's story is a volunteer spotlight. Impact stories about beneficiaries, such as Amina, Dr. Ravi, and Sophia, sit in a separate sister pillar called AI for Good. Same craft, different lane. Keeping those labels clean is what keeps a content calendar honest about whose story is being told and why.

## Key takeaways

- The Overcoming the Monster narrative arc maps directly onto real volunteer stories: struggle, turning point, contribution, resolution.
- A volunteer spotlight centers the volunteer's own journey and should read distinctly from a product or program pitch.
- The five-point checklist for any spotlight: open on a scene, show real change, stay human, end soft, get consent first.
- Consent must be secured before publishing, and a privacy level should be marked before a post goes out.
- Volunteer spotlights and beneficiary impact stories (the AI for Good pillar) are the same craft applied in two distinct, clearly labeled lanes.

Content and communications teams at nonprofits or humanitarian organizations writing volunteer or impact stories, who want a repeatable, ethical structure for turning a real person's experience into a story that respects them.
