A folder called Finances sits on a lot of solo operators' desktops, twelve months of revenue inside it, never opened. Not because the numbers are unanswerable, but because opening the file means formulas, and formulas mean guessing at syntax and still not trusting the answer that comes out. Claude's data plugin removes that step entirely: point Claude at the same spreadsheet and ask a question the way you'd ask a colleague, and it explores, cleans, visualizes, and compares the numbers back to you in plain sentences.

## The loop the plugin replaces

Without help, understanding your own numbers tends to follow a familiar, unproductive loop: export a CSV, open it, stare at rows and columns, get a vague sense that things are up or down, and close the file. The questions that actually matter, whether a client is fading, whether the mix has shifted, whether the business made money last month, go unanswered not because they're unanswerable but because answering felt too hard. Decisions end up riding on gut feeling instead of the data sitting right there.

## Asking plainly, no formula required

The alternative is asking directly, the same way you'd ask a colleague sitting across the desk: which clients bring in the most revenue, and has the mix changed over the last two quarters? No formula, no pivot table. Claude reads the data, runs the analysis, and hands back a sentence you can act on, faster than it takes to write a single SUM formula. That covers straightforward questions too: total sales by month, which client led last quarter, whether software spending has crept up over six months. Each question gets turned into the right analysis and answered in a sentence, not a dump of raw numbers.

That kind of question can turn a vague feeling into something specific and actionable. "I think business is going okay" becomes "three clients carry 60 percent of revenue, and one has been sliding since September," which is the real gap between reacting to a problem after it's already hurt you and seeing it coming early enough to do something about it.

## Four kinds of work

Beyond just answering questions, the plugin does four distinct kinds of work: exploration, cleaning, visualization, and comparison. Not every question needs all four, but together they turn a raw export into something you can actually trust and act on.

Exploration comes first: point Claude at an unfamiliar export with column headers that don't quite make sense, and it describes the structure, flags outliers, and suggests questions worth asking, often surfacing a better question than the one you started with. Cleaning handles the tedious reality that messy data is the norm rather than the exception: inconsistent date formats, duplicate rows, impossible values like negative sales. The plugin does that work and reports exactly what it changed, which is what actually lowers the barrier to working with your own numbers in the first place. Visualization turns a request for monthly revenue over the past year into an actual chart, a line chart for trends or bars for comparisons, ready for a deck, a partner conversation, or just your own sense of where the business is heading. Comparison is what separates a snapshot from a trend: how this quarter stacks against last, whether acquisition cost is climbing or falling. Totals tell you where you are; comparisons tell you where you're heading.

## Making it a monthly ritual

Every business has a question it's been avoiding, and the plugin is built for exactly that kind of question. Point Claude at your invoices and expenses and ask directly, "am I profitable month to month?" It reads both files, correlates them, and gives you a real read on financial health, often surfacing more in a five-minute conversation than a year of meaning to look ever would. The suggested habit is to make it a ritual: on the first of the month, spend ten minutes pointing Claude at last month's revenue and expenses and asking for a health check, total revenue, total expenses, margin, how it compares to the month before, and anything that looks off. Doing that consistently means each review stacks on the last, until a trend becomes visible well before it turns into a real problem.

Revenue concentration is one specific question worth asking directly for any service business: who are your top clients, what share rides on the top three, and is anyone quietly spending less than before? If a single client makes up 40 percent of your income and you don't know it, you can't plan for the day they leave, and the plugin makes that check trivial to run, along with cheap follow-ups: of the top clients, which are declining, and what changed last quarter that might explain it?

## What it can and can't tell you

The honest caveat runs through the whole approach: the plugin's analysis is only as trustworthy as two things, the data fed into it and the assumptions underneath it. Clean inputs and sound assumptions produce a reliable read; bad inputs produce confident, well-formatted nonsense. That's exactly why the plugin reports what it cleaned rather than cleaning silently, so the change log becomes an audit trail you can verify before trusting anything built on top of it. And there's a hard limit worth naming directly: the plugin can tell you a client is declining, but it can't tell you whether to call them, offer a discount, or let them go. It surfaces the number; the judgment stays yours.
