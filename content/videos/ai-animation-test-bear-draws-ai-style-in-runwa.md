Not every illustration style survives the jump from a still image to AI-generated motion. This animation test takes the sketchy, ink-heavy "Bear Draws AI" style, built on line work, geometry, and character exaggeration, and runs it through image-to-video tools to see what actually holds up and what falls apart.

## Why this style is a hard test case

The Bear Draws AI look is visually distinctive precisely because it relies on flatness and dense line detailing rather than smooth shading or simple shapes. That's great for editorial cartoons or conceptual illustration on a static page, but it's a difficult input for AI video tools like Runway, Veo3, or Kling, which need to interpret a still image and guess how its parts should move over time. Dense, flat line art gives these models less visual information to work with when they're deciding what's foreground, what's background, and how a limb should bend.

## What actually broke

The test surfaces a few consistent problems. Many of the stills showed flickering once animated, along with limbs that appeared to break or geometry that seemed to melt during motion. In some cases, character expressions warped or proportions collapsed entirely partway through a clip. These aren't edge cases unique to one image; they're the expected failure modes when a style built on fine detail and flat color gets fed into a model trained to infer depth and movement from more conventional imagery.

## What helps

The test points to a few practical fixes rather than treating the problem as unsolvable. Redesigning characters with clearer silhouettes tends to help, since a model has an easier time animating a shape it can clearly separate from its background. Simplifying the style, using cleaner outlines and larger contrast zones, also tends to animate more smoothly than dense, intricate linework. On the technical side, Runway in particular tends to drop resolution significantly during generation, so upscaling the finished clip with a tool like Topaz or Magnific AI is worth doing as a follow-up step to restore sharpness and detail.

## The bigger point about testing

The through-line of this video isn't "avoid this art style," it's "test before you commit." Animation tests exist to answer a narrower question than a finished piece does: does this specific visual translate into motion, and if not, where does it break? That's a cheap question to ask early and an expensive one to discover late, after a project has already been storyboarded around an art style that turns out not to animate cleanly.

## Key takeaways

- Flat, line-heavy art styles are more prone to flickering and warping in AI image-to-video tools than styles with clear silhouettes and contrast.
- Clean outlines and larger contrast zones tend to produce smoother AI-generated motion than dense detail.
- Runway can noticeably lower output resolution, so plan on an upscaling pass with a tool like Topaz or Magnific AI.
- Treat animation tests as prototypes, not final work, and run them before committing a project to a specific art style.
