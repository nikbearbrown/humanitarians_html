Most tutorials about AI tools promise a shortcut: type a prompt, get a result, move on. Professor Nik Bear Brown opens the OZ Songs project with a different premise entirely. This is a public, ongoing journal of building a personal reimagining of the Wizard of Oz with AI and human craft together, documented one lesson at a time, precisely because the tools change so fast that a single polished tutorial goes stale within months.

## A daily journal, not a finished course

The plan is not one big tutorial but a running series: short videos, three to five minutes, each teaching one specific thing as it comes up in the actual work. That might mean showing how to use a particular design tool, how to prompt effectively, how to build a custom GPT, or how to clone a voice, whatever the OZ Songs project happens to require that day. Rather than mapping out a fixed curriculum in advance, the series is structured as a working journal: build something, then make a video showing how it was done. Because Professor Brown teaches roughly five AI-related classes a semester at Northeastern, often involving games and art alongside more technical material, these same videos double as teaching resources for his students, who ask the same practical questions, how do I clone my voice, how do I composite an image, how do I write an agent, that this series is built to answer.

## Building a consistent character

A recurring thread in the demonstration is Dorothy, redesigned as an original character rather than a direct copy of the classic book or film. The process is shown as genuinely iterative: an early version of her boots got swapped out for a pair of red Timberland boots because that better fit the desired look, and the same character was then placed in a poppy field background, with the point being that once a character design is locked in, it can be dropped into new scenes and settings while staying recognizably the same character. The video is explicit that this is a personal creative reinterpretation, not a faithful retelling. In the original book, Dorothy wears silver shoes and is roughly fifteen years old; the famous ruby slippers came from the film adaptation. This version keeps the red footwear but as Timberland boots, and ages the character up to around thirty, changes made deliberately in the spirit of making it a personal version of the story. Because the original Wizard of Oz material is in the public domain, adapting it this freely is part of a long tradition, one that has already produced numerous other reinterpretations of the same source material.

## AI as a craft, not a shortcut

The video draws a direct comparison between AI work and writing: almost anyone can pick up a pencil and start a poem or a novel, but that alone does not make someone a working novelist or poet. Real novelists, poets, and songwriters refine their work repeatedly and care about the details, and that same discipline is what separates a quick AI output from something genuinely good. Typing a single prompt and accepting the first result will usually produce something merely okay. Treating AI generation like a craft, iterating, refining, and caring about the specific choices being made, is what the series is actually trying to teach, more than any single tool's interface.

## Voice cloning with real stakes

Among the specific techniques mentioned, voice cloning carries the most personal weight. Professor Brown describes having cloned his own voice repeatedly for this kind of work, and mentions a striking example: taking an old recording of his father and using it to create a voice model so his father could speak again. It is offered as a concrete illustration of what these tools make possible, not as an abstract feature demo, and it is the kind of application that connects directly to why a project like this matters beyond entertainment value.
