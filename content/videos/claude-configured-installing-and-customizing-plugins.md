Installing a plugin is the easy part. Click install, wait a few seconds, and it's active. But what most people miss is that the version that just installed is running entirely on generic defaults, not on anything specific to your business, and the real work of making it useful happens somewhere else entirely: a conversation, not a settings panel.

## Browsing and installing

Plugins live in a library made up of an official collection plus community additions. Each listing describes what it does and what it covers, so browsing by function is the way to find the ones that actually fit your work. Clicking install downloads and activates the plugin with no setup wizard involved. Once it's in, its capabilities simply appear: relevant skills switch on, slash commands show up in the menu, and connectors sit ready to be configured whenever you need them. If a colleague shares a plugin, or you find one from the community, it arrives as a file. Clicking upload and picking that file installs it the same way, in the same couple of minutes as any other install.

## Why the default isn't enough

Out of the box, a plugin runs on sensible, generic defaults, best practice for its general domain. That's useful as a starting point, but it isn't tailored to how your business actually works, your own process, your own terminology, your own way of doing things. Generic advice and a specific, workable output are two different things, and defaults only get you the first one.

## A conversation, not a configuration panel

Clicking customize doesn't open a settings screen with toggles and dropdowns. It starts a plain conversation. The plugin asks about your workflow, your preferences, your tools, and you answer in ordinary language, not structured fields. From there, the pattern repeats: it asks, you answer, it calibrates, and if you keep going, it asks again and tightens further. That loop is the same whether you spend two questions on it or twenty. How far you take it is entirely your call. Running on the defaults is still useful. Spending a few extra minutes gets you something that feels built specifically for your business, and the right amount of effort scales with how central that plugin's domain is to your actual day-to-day work.

## A worked example: a web design studio

Take a marketing plugin, installed and then customized. The first question is direct: what does your business do, and who are your customers? A real answer might be: a small web design studio, whose clients are professional services firms with no in-house marketing team of their own, accountants, lawyers, financial advisers who need a modern site but no dedicated marketing staff. A few more answers lock in the rest: a voice that's professional but approachable, no jargon, and formats limited to social posts and a monthly newsletter.

Once those answers are in, the plugin's output changes accordingly. Ask for a content calendar afterward and you get posts and newsletter topics built for that studio's actual clients, not viral video scripts or press releases aimed at a different kind of business entirely. A competitive scan targets other web design studios, not enterprise agencies several sizes too large to be a relevant comparison. Generic advice becomes advice that sounds like it comes from someone who actually knows the business.

## Self-contained versus connected

Some plugins are self-contained and useful out of the box without connecting to anything else. Others become dramatically more useful the moment you connect them to tools you already run. A sales plugin researching a prospect from public information alone gives a decent starting point; connect it to your CRM and it pulls in your actual relationship history and past conversations with that prospect. The same pattern holds for data: give a plugin files, or connect it to your live database, and it works directly with your real numbers instead of whatever you happen to hand it manually. Connecting follows a standard login flow, the same kind of authorization you'd use linking any other app, and it's entirely optional. Starting without connections and adding them later, once you can see the value, is a reasonable way to go.

## Disabling without losing anything

Disabling a plugin doesn't erase what you taught it. Your customization stays preserved, waiting, and flipping the plugin back on restores it exactly as it was, with nothing lost in between. Plugins also keep improving over time on their own, features get added and issues get fixed as part of regular updates, so it's worth checking in occasionally to see what's changed. In practice, most people keep two or three plugins active at once, with the rest staying installed but disabled, ready the moment a job calls for them; loading up on more plugins than that doesn't slow anything down or cause confusion.

## One caveat: it's local to your machine

There's one detail worth knowing clearly: plugins and everything you've taught them live on your machine specifically. Work from a second computer, and that setup starts from zero there; the customization is personal to the device and doesn't follow you automatically. After installing and customizing, it's worth running one direct test, typing a slash to see the plugin's available commands, or simply asking it to do a real job, build a content calendar for next month, for example. A properly tuned plugin answers in a noticeably more structured, business-aware way than the defaults did.

## Key takeaways

- Installing a plugin takes seconds and puts it on generic defaults; customizing it is a separate step that happens through conversation, not a settings panel.
- The customize conversation follows a repeatable loop: it asks, you answer, it calibrates, and you can stop after a couple of questions or keep tightening further.
- Answers about your business, clients, voice, and formats directly reshape future outputs, like content calendars and competitive scans, to match your actual work.
- Connecting a plugin to a CRM or live database lets it work with your real relationship history or real numbers instead of generic public information.
- Disabling a plugin preserves its customization; re-enabling it restores everything exactly as it was.
- Plugin customization lives locally on your machine and doesn't carry over automatically to a second computer.
