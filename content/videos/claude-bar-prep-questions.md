A law student prepping for the bar needs volume, hundreds of practice questions in the exact style of the real exam, each with a worked explanation. Ask Claude for a batch and it produces them in minutes: a fact pattern, four answer choices, and an explanation for why the correct answer wins. This video explains why that format matching the real exam doesn't mean the underlying rule is as reliable as certified bar-review material, and what to check before trusting one.

## Why exam-style formatting is tempting to trust

Claude's practice questions read exactly like a real bar question, correct structure, plausible wrong answers, confident exam-ready prose. That similarity makes it easy to assume the rule stated inside the explanation carries the same reliability as a certified bar-review company's material. The video's core argument is that this assumption doesn't hold automatically, reading like the exam and being checked against a current, correct rule are two separate things, and format alone doesn't establish the second one.

## How Claude actually generates these questions

Claude drafts a bar-prep question from the general patterns of legal writing it has learned, how a fact pattern reads, how a plausible wrong answer sounds, not from a live, licensed question bank that a bar-review company keeps current against real statutes and case law. That distinction matters because a licensed question bank is maintained and verified against current law as a product requirement, while Claude's draft is a generative exercise built on patterns rather than a checked source.

## The hearsay example

The video anchors its point with a concrete case: a practice question testing an exception to the hearsay rule. It names the exception, states a rule for it, and reasons through the fact pattern, all in confident, exam-ready prose. That confident presentation holds whether the rule inside it is accurate, blended together with a different exception, or built on a standard that's since been superseded. Nothing in the tone or polish of the writing would reveal which of those is actually the case.

## Confidence and hedging are both just style

The video makes a second, related point: the reverse also holds. An explanation that hedges, saying a rule may depend on jurisdiction, isn't a sign the rule is wrong either. Confidence and hedging are both stylistic features of the writing, neither one tells you whether the stated rule matches a real, current source. Only checking the rule against that source, a statute, a case, a current bar-review text, actually resolves the question.

## Key takeaways

- Claude can generate volume, exam-style bar prep questions quickly, but the format matching a real exam doesn't certify the legal accuracy of the rule inside it.
- Claude drafts these questions from general legal writing patterns, not from a licensed, currently maintained question bank.
- A confidently written rule statement can still be blended with a different exception or based on a superseded standard.
- Hedged language in an explanation doesn't indicate the rule is wrong, just as confident language doesn't indicate it's right.
- The only way to know if a stated rule is accurate is to check it against a real, current source yourself.
