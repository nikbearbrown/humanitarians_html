Most AI video content clogging up social feeds right now follows the same formula: something impossible happens in a hyper-real setting, a bear kisses a kid, a tiger fights a dog, and it's designed purely to stop your thumb for three seconds. Unreal Reels is a direct response to that pattern, built on a simple observation: the same technology that produces convincing fake footage can just as easily be pointed at telling an actual story.

## What the tool does

Unreal Reels is a custom GPT that takes a story or a set of song lyrics as input and generates a sequence of numbered prompts, styled to produce realistic, "gritty iPhone footage" style AI video clips. The tool is built around the idea that this realistic, handheld aesthetic, the kind of found-footage look you'd get from someone actually filming with their phone, is well suited to genuine narrative storytelling rather than random, disconnected spectacle.

## Why the prompts are numbered

The numbering system solves a specific, practical problem. Most text-to-video and text-to-image tools use the prompt text itself as the file title, which makes it hard to reassemble a sequence of clips into the correct order after generation. Unreal Reels prepends a nonsense sequence marker, something like A1 or A2, to each prompt specifically because it carries no semantic meaning the AI model will try to interpret. The model skips over it and builds the rest of the scene normally, while the creator gets clips that sort correctly by filename. It's a small trick, but it's the kind of detail that only shows up after actually trying to manage dozens of generated clips for a single project.

## The Night Before Christmas as a proof of concept

To demonstrate the tool, an adaptation of "'Twas the Night Before Christmas" was built as if someone were following Santa around with an iPhone: a sequence of random-feeling, found-footage-style clips that nonetheless tell the complete, recognizable story. That example does double duty. It shows the tool working end to end, and it demonstrates the core aesthetic idea, that "unreal" footage can still deliver a coherent, real narrative if the prompts are built with sequencing and story structure in mind.

## Two additional shooting styles

Beyond the plain gritty iPhone default, Unreal Reels includes two more specific style options. "Tiffany" is modeled on the elegant look of Breakfast at Tiffany's, and comes with an extensive set of rules covering camera angles, color choices, and the types of cameras it should evoke. "Colorful" produces a much more vibrant, saturated cinematography style. Both are framed as answering the same underlying question: what would this footage look like if a professional photographer, rather than a random passerby, were shooting it on an iPhone. More styles are planned beyond these two.

## A vision beyond single videos

The longer-term idea behind the tool points toward interactive storytelling, where a viewer chooses their own perspective within a story rather than watching a fixed sequence someone else edited together. The example given is navigating something like The Wizard of Oz through your own iPhone point of view, deciding in the moment what to look at and explore, rather than following a director's predetermined sequence. That's framed as a future direction rather than something the tool does today, but it explains why the sequencing and prompt-generation approach was built the way it was.
