There's something almost architectural about a cumulative song. Each verse doesn't just add a new line, it rebuilds the entire structure from the bottom up, forcing the singer to hold every previous layer in mind while adding one more. That structural demand is exactly what makes "There's a Hole in the Bottom of the Sea" such a good vehicle for this rough-cut music video, which pairs the song's escalating repetition with an underwater world that grows just as steadily.

## A world built layer by layer

The video's premise mirrors the song's structure directly. Starting from a simple hole at the bottom of the sea, each new verse introduces another element: a log in the hole, a branch on the log, a bump on the branch, a frog on the bump, and on through a tail, a speck, and a flick, each addition nested inside the one before it. Visually, the project brings this to life with hundreds of sea creatures populating an underwater ecosystem, ranging from tiny plankton up through majestic whales, with recognizable characters like Patrick, Gary the snail, and Squidward appearing as the layers deepen. Every new verse effectively adds another layer to the visual world in the same way it adds another layer to the song itself.

## Why the repetition matters

Cumulative songs like this one work because they force active recall rather than passive listening. Every time a new element is introduced, the singer has to repeat the entire chain that came before it, from the hole down through the log, the branch, the bump, the frog, and further. That structure isn't just charming as entertainment, it's functionally a memory exercise disguised as a nursery rhyme, and it's exactly the kind of repetition-driven engagement that the Lyrical Literacy research this project is based on points to as valuable.

## The science behind the approach

This project draws on Lyrical Literacy research from Humanitarians AI, which points to music as an activity that engages more regions of the brain than almost any other activity people do regularly. The claim isn't just that singing is fun, it's that singing along to something like a cumulative song can boost memory, strengthen language skills, and support broader cognitive function. A song like this one, where each verse demands recall of everything that came before, is a particularly clean example of that principle in action: the singer isn't just listening, they're actively rebuilding a growing structure in their head every time a new verse starts.

## A rough cut, not a finished product

This version of the video is explicitly presented as a first pass rather than a finished piece, and the creator is actively looking for feedback before locking in a final version. Specific questions posed to viewers include which sea creatures stood out, how the layered storytelling felt as it built up verse by verse, what viewers would add or change, and whether the video succeeded in making people want to sing along. That last question in particular gets at the real goal of the project: it's not enough for the video to look good, it needs to actually pull viewers into singing, since the singing itself is where the cognitive benefit lives.
