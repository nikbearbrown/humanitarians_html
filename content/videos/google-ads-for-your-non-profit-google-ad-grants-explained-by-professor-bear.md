Ten thousand dollars a month in free advertising sounds almost too good to be true for a nonprofit, and in a sense, the catch isn't the money, it's staying compliant enough to keep it. Professor Bear opens this series on Google Ad Grants with exactly that tension: a genuinely valuable program that's easy to lose if you don't understand what Google actually wants from your organization's website.

## What Google Ad Grants actually offers

The core of the program is $10,000 a month in AdSense credit, money nonprofits can put toward driving traffic and awareness without spending their own budget on ads. On top of that, qualifying organizations get access to Google Workspace, which brings real practical value on its own: proper organizational email addresses, generous storage, and the collaboration tools that come with a paid Workspace account, all at no cost. For a nonprofit trying to stretch a limited budget, that combination is significant.

## Why it's trickier than it looks

Despite holding a PhD in computer science, Professor Bear is candid that Humanitarians AI still made mistakes with the program, mistakes serious enough to get the account deactivated for a period of time. Getting back into compliance took real effort: understanding exactly what Google wanted from the organization's site and its operations, rather than assuming good intentions were enough. That experience is the entire reason this series exists. The goal is to walk other nonprofits through the same territory so they don't repeat the same errors.

## Why Google cares so much about compliance

The reason Google enforces these requirements as strictly as it does comes down to legitimacy. Google wants confidence that a grant recipient is a genuinely legitimate nonprofit doing real work, not a site using the credit to funnel traffic toward something closer to spam. To establish that confidence, they want a substantial amount of evidence on your website itself: proof that you're actually doing something for your stated cause, not just claiming to. Meeting that bar isn't trivial. It requires the site to demonstrate its mission clearly and consistently across many different touchpoints.

## The checklist approach

To manage the sheer number of requirements, Professor Bear and his team built a checklist, does the website do this, does the website do that, walking through Google's expectations item by item. He describes it as fairly extensive and genuinely not simple to satisfy in full. Rather than treat that complexity as a reason to avoid the program, the series exists to break the checklist down piece by piece, sharing what Humanitarians AI has learned firsthand from working inside the program, while being clear that these are personal observations rather than official statements on Google's behalf.

## Using AI tools to manage the requirements

Given how many individual requirements are involved, Professor Bear points out that using AI tools to help track and manage compliance becomes close to necessary rather than optional. With that many boxes to check across a website, keeping everything aligned by hand is a real burden, and AI assistance is part of how the team has kept up with it.

## Why the site was built on Vercel and React

A meaningful part of staying compliant, in Professor Bear's view, comes down to the technical foundation of the website itself. Google wants a site that's fast, doesn't throw page errors, and behaves reliably for visitors. That's part of why Humanitarians AI built its site on a platform like Vercel using React, which gives more control over performance and reliability, including access to edge computing. Edge computing means web pages get served from whichever location is physically closest to a given visitor's request, rather than always routing through one centralized server, which improves load times and reliability across a distributed audience. Upcoming videos in the series will go deeper into how the site was built on Vercel and React specifically, and the underlying code for the Humanitarians AI website is available for anyone to download and use as a reference.

## The core theme: transparency

If there's one idea Professor Bear keeps returning to, it's transparency. Google wants to see clearly what an organization is about and who it is, presented plainly across the site. Organizations that don't make that clear risk exactly what happened to Humanitarians AI: deactivation, not out of any intent to deceive, but simply from not understanding what Google was actually asking for. Beyond the core eligibility questions, the checklist also covers things like a privacy policy, a cookie policy, and terms of service. None of these are strictly required by Google, but Professor Bear considers them good practice regardless, along with making sure that any donation flow on the site is secure and trustworthy.
