Agrima, a Humanitarians AI fellow, draws on her experience working on the LON conservation project to explain a pattern she kept seeing repeated: AI is quietly solving one of the oldest problems in nonprofit marketing, without much fanfare. The video is not about a flashy new tool. It's about where AI is actually landing inside teams that were never staffed to keep up with the work expected of them.

## The resource gap small nonprofit teams live with

Nonprofit marketing routinely asks a five-person organization to handle the same donor outreach, storytelling, and campaign work as a company with a much larger team and a fraction of the budget, often with just one person covering several roles at once. Agrima saw this directly on the LON project, where marketing largely meant figuring things out alongside the actual conservation work, because there was never a separate team to hand it off to. She's clear that this isn't a complaint; it's the baseline reality most small teams operate inside, and it's the gap she argues AI is starting to close.

## Adoption is already past the early-adopter stage

More than half of nonprofits are now piloting or actively using AI tools, and about 30% report that AI has directly increased their fundraising revenue over the past year. The tasks getting automated first are the ones that used to consume the most staff time for the least strategic value: drafting donor emails, turning an impact report into social media posts, summarizing board meeting notes, and repurposing one long piece of content into several shorter ones. None of this is exciting work, Agrima notes, but it all used to require someone sitting down and doing it by hand.

## Where personalization changes outcomes, not just speed

The part Agrima finds most interesting is donor personalization, because it's where AI stops being just a timesaver and starts genuinely improving results. Emails personalized to reflect what a specific donor actually cares about, rather than a generic message sent to the whole list, reportedly double open and click-through rates. Even something as simple as an AI-optimized donation form shows a measurable lift: average donations around $161 compared to an industry-wide average of $15. She frames that gap as tied to specific, believable causes: better timing, clearer language, and less friction at the moment someone decides to give.

## What AI still can't do

Agrima is careful to push back on the idea that AI simply fixes nonprofit marketing. AI can draft a donor email quickly, but it can't decide what the organization actually wants to say or whether the message is honest. That judgment call stays entirely human, and she argues it should. Her broader point is that this isn't a story about big technology reshaping fundraising from the top down. It's closer to the opposite: a set of tools, mostly free or offered at a nonprofit discount, that happen to help most exactly where resources are thinnest, not because anyone designed it that way, but because that's where the need was greatest.

## Why it matters for a project like LON

For a small, mission-driven team like the one behind the LON project, this isn't a minor convenience. Agrima describes it as often the difference between having a real public presence and not having one at all, which for a project that size is the whole ball game.
