Applying for a corporate nonprofit technology program should be simple when you meet every listed requirement. Upload your IRS determination letter, confirm your 501(c)(3) status, and wait for approval. That is not what happened when Humanitarians AI applied for Microsoft's nonprofit program.

## What the eligibility guidelines actually said

The published requirements were straightforward: organizations verified as a 501(c)(3) qualify. Humanitarians AI is a verified 501(c)(3), and the application included the organization's IRS determination letter as proof. By the letter of Microsoft's own stated rules, the application should have cleared review without issue.

## The rejection, without an explanation

Instead, the application came back denied, with no clear reason given for why an organization that met the stated criteria was found ineligible. There was no specific gap identified, no request for additional documentation, and no indication of what, if anything, was missing from the submission. The IRS letter was there. The 501(c)(3) status was there. The eligibility box was checked. And yet the answer was no.

## Why this matters beyond one rejected form

A rejection like this is frustrating on its own, but it points to a bigger problem for small nonprofits trying to access corporate technology programs. These programs are often positioned as a straightforward benefit for verified nonprofits, but the review process behind them isn't always transparent. When an application is denied without explanation, an organization has no way to know whether it was a documentation issue, a processing error, or something else entirely. That uncertainty makes it hard to know whether appealing is worth the time, or whether the better use of limited staff hours is to look elsewhere for the technology support the organization needs.

## Deciding whether to appeal

The immediate question after a rejection like this is whether to pursue it further: email the program, ask for clarification, and push for a real answer, or treat the denial as final and move on. Neither option is free. An appeal takes time and staff attention that a small nonprofit doesn't have in abundance, with no guarantee the outcome changes. Walking away means giving up on a program that, on paper, the organization qualifies for.

## Key takeaways

- Meeting a program's published eligibility criteria, including submitting the required IRS documentation, does not guarantee approval.
- Rejections that arrive without a stated reason leave applicants unable to diagnose or fix the actual problem.
- Nonprofits should keep copies of everything submitted, including determination letters, in case an appeal becomes necessary.
- A lack of transparency in vendor review processes is itself a cost that small organizations have to absorb.
- Weighing whether to appeal means weighing staff time against the uncertain odds of a different outcome.
