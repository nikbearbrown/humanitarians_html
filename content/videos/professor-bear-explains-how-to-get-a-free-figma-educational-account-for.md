Figma offers a completely free educational account to verified students and teachers, giving access to both Figma and FigJam at no cost. The pitch is simple. The actual verification process, walked through live here, turns out to be a little more of a maze than the marketing suggests, and seeing exactly what it asks for is more useful than reading a features page.

## Starting the verification

The process begins at Figma's education page by clicking "Get verified." From there, applicants choose a role, and in this case, someone who qualifies as both a student and an educator selects "educator, higher ed" simply because it seemed like the more interesting option to test. The next fields ask for a school name (in this case, Northeastern University, selected from what appears to be a limited dropdown list of recognized institutions), followed by a primary field of study or teaching, and then a request for supporting documentation: a copy of a class syllabus.

## The documentation hurdle

This is where the process gets more demanding than a simple ".edu" email check. Figma asks for a copy of a class syllabus, a class schedule, and proof of enrollment for students, or comparable teaching materials for educators. None of these documents were prepared in advance, so the workaround was to grab whatever syllabus happened to be on hand, in this case, one for a course on computational skepticism, and upload it along with a URL for the course being taught. Since not every course has a public URL, a GitHub link was substituted instead.

## A verification process that doesn't verify much

The most notable observation from the walkthrough is how little the uploaded documents actually seem to get checked. The syllabus submitted was not even the correct one for the course claimed, and the URL provided was a GitHub link rather than an actual course page, yet after agreeing to the terms and submitting, the account came back verified almost immediately, with confirmation arriving by email. The process asks for a fair amount of friction, syllabus uploads, schedule proof, enrollment documentation, but nothing in the actual outcome suggested any of it was cross-checked against a real institution's records. That gap between the apparent rigor of the ask and the apparent looseness of the check is the real story of the video: legitimate students and teachers end up jumping through hoops that don't appear to filter out anyone determined to get through them.

## Practical guidance for anyone applying

For a student or teacher who actually wants to get verified quickly, the practical lesson is straightforward: have some kind of syllabus or course document ready before starting, even if it is not a perfect match for what you are claiming, and have a URL, whether a real course page or a GitHub repository, ready to paste in. The dropdown school list is worth checking in advance too, since verification depends on your institution appearing in Figma's recognized list. Beyond that, the process does not appear to require perfection in the supporting documents, just something that fills each required field.
