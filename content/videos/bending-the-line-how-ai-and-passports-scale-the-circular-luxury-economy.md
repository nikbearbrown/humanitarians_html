Liam, in for KMO, argues that circular luxury is not fundamentally a sustainability story, it is an identity problem. You cannot recirculate a bag if you cannot prove it is real, and the whole case for AI plus digital product passports rests on solving that.

## Why the old luxury chain does not loop on its own

The traditional luxury chain is a straight line: design, cut, sell, forget. A circular economy bends that line into a loop through resale, repair, and reuse instead of the item ending up in a landfill. Liam is direct that this loop only works if the object stays legible: authentic, priced, and findable. Houses like presenting the loop on a slide, but the operational question they avoid is who proves a given jacket is real and who actually captures the value of the second sale.

## The authentication bottleneck

A used bag without a receipt is, in Liam's framing, just a rumor. Counterfeits poison the resale price of the real thing, and human authenticators cannot inspect every listing at the volume secondhand luxury needs. The loop stalls at trust until the object can effectively identify itself. Without that, circularity is, as Liam puts it, a donation bin with better lighting.

## Inside Vestiaire Collective's hybrid verification stack

Liam uses Vestiaire Collective as the worked example of how this bottleneck gets addressed in practice: over 150 experts across five hubs working alongside machine learning inside the verification stack. In 2024, roughly ten percent of listings failed digital checks that combine image analysis, condition assessment, and counterfeit detection in the same filter. The key point is that AI multiplies what the expert can cover, it does not replace the expert's judgment.

## Europe's digital product passport timeline

The second half of the mechanism is the record itself. Europe is adding a digital product passport requirement covering materials, origin, care, and end of life, reached through a data carrier on the product. Textiles are a priority category, and the European Commission's textile-specific rules are planned for late 2027, not an overnight 2026 switch. Liam notes that a passport can meaningfully raise an item's lifetime value, and that roughly ninety percent of brands still see this compliance requirement as red tape rather than an opportunity.

## Telling theater from mechanism

Liam closes with a practical distinction: a green lookbook and a takeback bin are theater unless a mechanism actually authenticates the object and lets it re-enter the market. If second-life sales volume does not rise, the circular story is marketing, full stop. The suggested exercise is to pick a luxury house, map its public circular claims around resale, repair, takeback, and recycled materials, then check whether it separately discusses authentication, digital product passports, or item-level traceability, scoring the gap between the two.

## Key takeaways

- Circular luxury resale is blocked primarily by an identity and trust problem, not a demand problem.
- Counterfeits lower resale value for authentic items and human authenticators alone cannot scale to inspect every listing.
- Vestiaire Collective's verification stack pairs machine learning with over 150 human experts rather than replacing expert judgment.
- Europe's digital product passport requirement for textiles is planned for late 2027, and most brands currently view it as a compliance burden.
- A circular claim is theater unless it is backed by a mechanism that actually authenticates items and grows second-life sales volume.
