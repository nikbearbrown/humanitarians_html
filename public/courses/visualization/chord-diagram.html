// LEARN — Chord Diagram

What this chart is

A chord diagram encodes bilateral relationships in a circular layout. Nodes sit on the circumference as arcs; their angular width is proportional to the total flow in or out of that entity. Ribbons connecting arcs are Bézier curves whose width at each endpoint encodes the directional magnitude of the flow — how much entity A sends to entity B is read from A's end of the ribbon, and vice versa. The perceptual mechanism is area: wider ribbons carry more. Two entities with a large mutual relationship dominate the visual field, making the strongest connections impossible to miss. The encoding is inherently relational — unlike a matrix or bar chart, the chord diagram places all pairwise relationships in simultaneous view.

Why it was chosen here

The data presents six entities with meaningful pairwise flows and the goal is revealing which relationships dominate, which are symmetric, and which are asymmetric. A heatmap matrix could show all 36 cells, but cannot encode magnitude as intuitively as area. A network node-link diagram can show topology but loses proportional flow without cumbersome edge-weight labels. The chord diagram exploits the fact that the human visual system is excellent at judging relative area, making it possible to rank relationships at a glance. With six nodes and fifteen unique ribbons, the layout remains legible — the critical threshold. At ten or more nodes, overcrowding degrades readability and the chart should be filtered or split.

What the alternative would break

A directed arrow network graph — the most common naive substitute — fails to communicate proportional flow. Edge thickness can encode weight, but edges overlap, cross, and the layout algorithm has no obligation to place high-weight edges prominently. The chord diagram solves this by construction: the arc ordering and radial layout guarantee that ribbon width is always legible against the neutral interior. The other tempting alternative, a sankey diagram, is excellent for flows between sequential stages but presupposes directionality and a left-to-right narrative. When relationships are bilateral and the "stages" don't exist, a sankey imposes false structure.

// FRAMEWORK

FT Visual Vocabulary category: Part-to-whole / Relationships — "Show how individual parts relate to each other and to the whole." Abela quadrant: Relationship (two or more variables, comparing magnitude of association). The one design decision worth knowing: arc gap size (here ~2°) controls the visual separation between nodes. Too small and arcs blur together; too large and the ribbons appear disconnected from their source. Two degrees per gap is Bostock's canonical default for six-node diagrams and was retained deliberately.
